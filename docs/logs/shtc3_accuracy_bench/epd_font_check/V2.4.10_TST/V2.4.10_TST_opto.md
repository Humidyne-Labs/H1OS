# Challenge Assessment & Mitigation [11/20/2026, 3:55pm]

The visibility and legibility of a tiny feature—like a 1-pixel-wide stroke of the letter `'s'` at $20\text{ cm}$—is governed by two foundational laws in optics and vision science: **Visual Angle (Snellen's Principle)** and the **Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF)** (along with **Ricco's Law** for tiny features).

Here is how those laws work and why a 1-pixel-wide `'s'` on your display becomes hard to make out at $20\text{ cm}$.

---

### 1. The Governing Laws & Rules

#### A. Law of Visual Angle (Snellen's Principle / Minimum Angle of Resolution)

Your eye does not measure things in millimeters or pixels; it measures **angular size ($\theta$)** on the retina:

$$\theta = 2 \arctan\left(\frac{S}{2D}\right) \approx \frac{S}{D} \quad (\text{in radians})$$

Where:

* $S$ = physical size of the feature (stroke width or gap)
* $D$ = viewing distance

**Snellen’s Rule (20/20 Vision Standard):**

* To recognize a letter (like `'s'` vs `'e'` or `'8'`), the **critical detail**—specifically the stroke thickness *and* the white gaps between the curves of the `'s'`—must subtend at least **1 arcminute** ($1/60^\circ \approx 0.000291\text{ rad}$) under ideal, high-contrast conditions.
* Consequently, a standard Snellen optotype letter (which is built on a $5 \times 5$ grid where each stroke and gap is $1/5$ of the letter height) must be at least **5 arcminutes tall** just to be barely decipherable, and **15–20 arcminutes tall** for comfortable reading.

#### B. The Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF) & Weber's Law

Snellen’s 1-arcminute rule assumes **near-infinite contrast (100:1+)** and **bright illumination**. In reality, human visual acuity drops sharply as contrast and reflectance decrease, governed by **Weber Contrast ($C_W$)**:

$$C_W = \frac{L_{\text{background}} - L_{\text{text}}}{L_{\text{background}}} = \frac{R_1 - R_d}{R_1} = 1 - \frac{1}{\text{CR}}$$

Where:

* $R_1$ = white reflectance
* $R_d$ = dark reflectance
* $\text{CR} = R_1 / R_d$ = Contrast Ratio

According to the **Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF)**, as spatial frequency increases (i.e., lines get thinner, approaching 1 pixel wide), the human eye requires **exponentially higher contrast** to detect the edge.

#### C. Ricco’s Law (Spatial Summation) & E-Ink Edge Bleed

For tiny objects near the resolution limit, **Ricco’s Law** states that the eye averages light across a small receptive field. If a single black pixel is surrounded by white pixels, optical scattering and physical microcapsule bleeding (ink spread between adjacent electrodes) reduce the effective contrast of a 1-pixel line compared to a 2-pixel line.

### 2. Step-by-Step Calculation for Your Scenario

Let's plug the exact numbers from your **1.54" e-Paper V2 datasheet** into these laws for a **1-pixel-wide `'s'**` at **$D = 20\text{ cm}$ ($200\text{ mm}$)**:

#### Step 1: Angular Size of 1 Pixel at $20\text{ cm}$

From the datasheet, the pixel pitch is $0.135\text{ mm}$:

$$\theta_{\text{1 pixel}} = \frac{0.135\text{ mm}}{200\text{ mm}} = 0.000675\text{ radians}$$

Converting radians to arcminutes ($\times \frac{180}{\pi} \times 60$):

$$\theta_{\text{1 pixel}} = 0.000675 \times 3437.75 \approx \mathbf{2.32\text{ arcminutes}}$$

#### Step 2: Why `'s'` Fails Even Though $2.32' > 1.0'$

Mathematically, a 1-pixel line ($2.32'$) is larger than the theoretical 1-arcminute limit. So why is a 1-pixel-wide `'s'` hard to read at $20\text{ cm}$? Three compounding penalties push it below your eye's visibility threshold:

1. **The Geometry of `'s'` (5 Vertical Elements):**
A lowercase `'s'` requires **three horizontal strokes** (top bar, middle crossbar, bottom bar) separated by **two white interior gaps** (counters).
* If the stroke is 1 pixel wide, the minimum height of an `'s'` is **5 pixels** ($0.675\text{ mm}$).
* At $20\text{ cm}$, a 5-pixel-tall `'s'` subtends only **$11.6\text{ arcminutes}$** total height. Because `'s'` is curved rather than blocky like `'E'`, diagonal pixels touch corner-to-corner, shrinking the internal white gaps to less than $0.135\text{ mm}$ ($< 1.5\text{ arcminutes}$)—causing the `'s'` to visually collapse into a blurry blob or look like `'5'`, `'a'`, or `'e'`.

2. **Low Reflectance & Contrast Ratio Penalty:**
Per your datasheet's Optical Characteristics:
* **White Reflectance ($R$):** $30\%\text{ min}$, $35\%\text{ typical}$ (meaning 65%–70% of room light is absorbed even by the "white" background).

* **Indoor Contrast Ratio ($\text{CR}$):** $10:1\text{ typical}$.

* That yields a Weber contrast of $1 - \frac{1}{10} = \mathbf{90\%}$, *before* any ambient glare or protective cover glass. In normal indoor lighting (e.g., 300–500 lux, whereas the datasheet's inspection standard uses $800\text{–}1300\text{ lux}$), a 35% reflective background looks light gray, shifting your eye's Contrast Sensitivity Function so that 1-pixel details require roughly **2.5 to 3 arcminutes** just to resolve cleanly.

3. **1-Bit Monochrome & Microcapsule Bleed:**
Because the display is strictly 1-bit B/W (no grayscale anti-aliasing), a 1-pixel curve has harsh stair-stepping. Furthermore, electrophoretic capsules slightly overlap pixel boundaries; a single isolated black row of pixels often measures closer to a **5:1 or 6:1 contrast ratio** rather than the full-area 10:1 spec.

### 3. How to Fix It (Engineering Rule of Thumb)

To make `'s'` effortlessly legible at **$20\text{ cm}$** on this $188\text{ DPI}$ panel:

* **Target Stroke Width:** At least **2 pixels** ($0.27\text{ mm} \rightarrow 4.64\text{ arcminutes}$ at $20\text{ cm}$).
* **Target Character Height:** At least **10 to 12 pixels** ($1.35\text{–}1.62\text{ mm} \rightarrow 23\text{–}28\text{ arcminutes}$ at $20\text{ cm}$), leaving at least 2–3 white pixels inside the upper and lower loops of the `'s'` so they don't close up.
